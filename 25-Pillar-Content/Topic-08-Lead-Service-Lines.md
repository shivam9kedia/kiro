## Introduction

An estimated **9.2 million lead service lines** still deliver drinking water to American homes, according to the EPA's most recent inventory. These are the pipes connecting your home to the water main beneath the street — and if yours is made of lead, every glass of water your family drinks passes through it.

Lead has no safe level of exposure. The CDC, EPA, and WHO all agree: any amount of lead in drinking water poses health risks, particularly for children under 6, pregnant women, and developing fetuses. Yet millions of homeowners don't know whether their service line contains lead.

This guide shows you exactly how to identify your service line material, explains the landmark EPA Lead and Copper Rule Revisions requiring nationwide replacement, connects you to utility lookup tools, and provides immediate interim protection strategies while replacement programs roll out.

## What Is a Lead Service Line?

A service line is the pipe that connects your home's internal plumbing to the public water main, typically running under your yard from the street to your basement or crawl space. Homes built before **1986** — when Congress banned lead in new plumbing installations — may have service lines made entirely of lead.

**Key facts:**
- Lead service lines are most common in homes built between **1900 and 1950**, though some installations continued through the mid-1980s
- The pipe connects at two points: the utility-side connection at the water main ("utility side") and the homeowner-side connection entering your house ("private side")
- In many municipalities, the homeowner is responsible for the private side of the service line
- Even homes with copper interior plumbing may have a lead service line underground — they're different systems

**Geographic concentration:** Lead service lines are most prevalent in the Midwest and Northeast, particularly in cities like Chicago (estimated 400,000 lines), Newark, Pittsburgh, Milwaukee, Detroit, and Cleveland. However, they exist in communities in all 50 states.

## How to Check If Your Home Has a Lead Service Line

You can often determine your pipe material without hiring a professional. Here's the step-by-step identification process:

### Method 1: Visual Inspection at Entry Point

1. **Locate where water enters your home** — typically in the basement, crawl space, or utility closet near the front of the house
2. **Find the pipe entering through the foundation wall** — this is your service line
3. **Examine the pipe material** using these tests:

**The Scratch Test:**
- Use a coin or flathead screwdriver to scratch an unpainted section of the pipe
- **Lead:** Reveals a shiny silver color; the metal is soft and scratches easily
- **Copper:** Reveals a penny-colored/reddish surface
- **Galvanized steel:** Dull gray, hard to scratch, often has threaded connections
- **Plastic (PVC/PE):** Obviously plastic, flexible

**The Magnet Test:**
- Hold a magnet to the pipe
- **Lead and copper:** Magnet will NOT stick
- **Galvanized steel:** Magnet WILL stick
- (If the magnet doesn't stick and the scratch test shows silver, it's almost certainly lead)

**Visual cues for lead pipes:**
- Dull gray color when unscratched
- Slightly bulging or swollen connections (lead was often wiped/soldered)
- Soft enough to dent with a fingernail or coin
- No threaded fittings (lead was joined with wiped joints)

### Method 2: Utility Service Line Inventory

The EPA now requires all water utilities to complete a service line material inventory. Contact your water utility for their records:
- Check your utility's website for an online lookup tool
- Call customer service with your address
- Many cities now have interactive maps showing service line materials by address

### Method 3: Your Home's Age and History

- Built before 1986: Lead service line is possible
- Built before 1950: Lead service line is probable in many regions
- Built after 1986: Lead service line is extremely unlikely (federally banned)

## The EPA Lead and Copper Rule Revisions (LCRR): What's Changing

The EPA's Lead and Copper Rule Improvements (LCRI), finalized in 2024, represents the most significant overhaul of lead regulation in 30 years:

**Key requirements:**
- **Mandatory inventory:** All water utilities must identify and publish the material of every service line in their system
- **Replacement mandate:** Utilities must replace all lead service lines within **10 years** of the rule's effective date
- **Full replacement required:** "Partial" replacements (replacing only the utility side) are no longer acceptable — both utility and private sides must be replaced
- **Action level reduction:** The lead action level drops from 15 parts per billion (ppb) to **10 ppb**
- **Tap sampling improvements:** More accurate testing protocols to better detect lead

**Timeline reality:**
- Inventory completion: Required by October 2024 (many systems still catching up)
- Replacement deadline: 10 years from rule effective date
- Estimated cost: $45–$60 billion nationally for full replacement

**What this means for homeowners:** Even with mandatory replacement, the 10-year timeline means millions of families will continue drinking from lead lines for years. Interim protection is essential during this transition.

## Utility Lookup Tools: Find Your Service Line Status

Several resources help you determine your service line material:

1. **Your water utility's website** — Search "[your city] lead service line map" or "[your utility] service line inventory"
2. **EPA's Lead Service Line Inventory Dashboard** — A national-level tool linking to individual utility inventories
3. **BlueConduit mapping** — Some cities use this AI-powered tool to predict service line materials
4. **State drinking water programs** — Your state's environmental or health department may maintain databases

**If your utility classifies your line as "unknown":** This often means they haven't physically verified the material. Request an inspection or perform the scratch/magnet test yourself at the entry point.

## Health Risks: Why Lead Demands Immediate Action

Lead exposure through drinking water is particularly dangerous because:

**For children:**
- Damages developing brains and nervous systems
- Reduces IQ and attention span
- Causes behavioral problems and learning disabilities
- Effects are **irreversible** — there is no treatment to undo lead damage to a child's brain

**For adults:**
- Increases blood pressure and hypertension risk
- Causes kidney damage with chronic exposure
- Reproductive harm (reduced fertility, increased miscarriage risk)
- Cardiovascular effects

**For pregnant women:**
- Lead crosses the placental barrier
- Associated with premature birth and low birth weight
- Can release stored bone lead during pregnancy, exposing the fetus even without current water lead exposure

**Concentration factors:**
- Lead levels are highest in **first-draw water** (water that's been sitting in pipes overnight)
- Hot water dissolves more lead than cold — never use hot tap water for drinking or cooking
- Homes with lead service lines typically show levels of **5–50 ppb** or higher in first-draw samples (the current action level is 15 ppb; no amount is safe)

## Interim Protection: Filtration While You Wait for Replacement

While advocating for and awaiting service line replacement, these filtration methods provide immediate protection:

### NSF/ANSI 53 Certified Filters (Verified Lead Removal)

Only filters specifically **NSF/ANSI 53 certified for lead reduction** are verified to remove lead. Look for this specific certification — not just "NSF certified" for taste and odor.

**Effective options:**
- **Point-of-use reverse osmosis:** >99% lead removal; most comprehensive protection
- **NSF 53-certified carbon block filters:** 99%+ lead removal when properly sized and maintained
- **NSF 53-certified pitcher filters:** Convenient but require frequent replacement and have limited capacity
- **NSF 53-certified faucet-mount filters:** Good balance of convenience and effectiveness

### Critical Maintenance Requirements

- Replace filters according to manufacturer schedule — lead removal capacity declines with use
- A filter past its rated capacity may pass lead through without any visible indication
- First-draw water (first use in morning) may overwhelm filter capacity — consider flushing lines for 30 seconds before filtering

### Additional Protective Practices

- Run cold water for 30–60 seconds before using for drinking or cooking (flushes standing water from lead pipes)
- Never use hot water from the tap for drinking, cooking, or baby formula (heat increases lead dissolution)
- Have your water tested by a certified laboratory — your utility may provide free testing

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if my home has lead pipes vs. lead solder?

Lead service lines are the underground pipes connecting your home to the water main — you can identify them at the entry point using the scratch test. Lead solder is different: it's the material used to join copper pipes at fittings, common in homes built between 1950–1986. Both contribute to lead in drinking water, but lead service lines typically contribute higher concentrations. A certified water test measures total lead regardless of source.

### Will a whole-house water filter remove lead?

Most whole-house sediment and carbon filters do NOT remove dissolved lead effectively. Lead removal requires specific media (KDF, activated alumina) or very dense carbon blocks specifically rated for lead. For verified lead protection, point-of-use RO systems or NSF 53-certified under-sink filters at drinking water taps are recommended rather than relying solely on whole-house treatment.

### Who pays for lead service line replacement?

Under the EPA's Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, water utilities must replace the full service line (both utility and private sides). Many municipalities now cover the full cost through ratepayer funding, federal infrastructure dollars, or state revolving funds. However, policies vary by location. Contact your utility to determine your financial responsibility, if any.

### How long does lead service line replacement take?

Individual line replacement typically takes 1–3 days of active construction. However, entering a utility's replacement queue may take months to years depending on demand and program capacity. During this waiting period, interim filtration provides immediate protection. After replacement, have your water retested — construction can temporarily disturb lead solder and sediment in interior plumbing.
