In April 2026, the Environmental Protection Agency officially added microplastics to the Contaminant Candidate List 6 (CCL 6), signaling that plastic particles in drinking water are now a federal regulatory priority. ## What Are Microplastics (and Nanoplastics)?

Microplastics are plastic fragments smaller than 5 millimeters in diameter. Nanoplastics are even tinier — less than 1 micrometer (1/1000th of a millimeter) — small enough to penetrate cell membranes and cross the blood-brain barrier.

**Where they come from:**
- Degradation of larger plastic waste (bags, bottles, packaging)
- Synthetic clothing fibers released during washing (a single load releases 700,000+ fibers)
- Tire wear particles washed into waterways
- Industrial processes and plastic manufacturing
- Breakdown of PVC water pipes and plastic plumbing components

**Why they're in your tap water:** Municipal water treatment plants were designed to remove bacteria, sediment, and chemical contaminants — not microscopic plastic particles. Studies consistently find that conventional treatment removes only **60–70%** of microplastics, meaning significant quantities pass through to distribution systems and ultimately your tap.

## The Columbia University Nanoplastics Study: What They Found

In January 2024, researchers at Columbia University published a landmark study in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* that transformed our understanding of plastic contamination in drinking water.

**Key findings:**
- Bottled water contains an average of **240,000 detectable plastic particles per liter** — 10 to 100 times more than previous estimates
- Approximately 90% of detected particles were **nanoplastics** (previously undetectable with older methods)
- Researchers used a new technique called stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) microscopy to detect particles as small as 100 nanometers
- Common plastics identified included PET (from bottles themselves), polyamide, polystyrene, polyvinyl chloride, and polymethyl methacrylate

**Why this matters for tap water:** If bottled water — which undergoes additional processing — contains this density of nanoplastics, tap water passing through miles of aging infrastructure (including plastic pipes) carries its own significant burden. Studies of tap water in major U.S. cities have detected an average of 5,000–10,000+ microplastic particles per liter, though nanoplastic counts may be far higher with newer detection methods.

## EPA CCL 6: What the Regulatory Designation Means

The Contaminant Candidate List is the EPA's official roster of unregulated contaminants that may require future regulation. Inclusion on CCL 6 means:

1. **Acknowledged concern:** The EPA formally recognizes microplastics as a potential drinking water health risk
2. **Research prioritization:** Federal funding will support occurrence studies and health effects research
3. **Future regulation possible:** CCL listing is the first step toward potential Maximum Contaminant Level (MCL) regulations

**The timeline reality:** Previous contaminants have taken 10–20+ years to move from CCL listing to enforceable regulation. PFAS chemicals were first identified as concerns in the early 2000s and didn't receive federal MCLs until 2024. Microplastics regulation could follow a similarly extended timeline.

**What this means for your family:** You cannot wait for government regulation to protect your household. The EPA's designation validates the concern, but actionable protection is your responsibility today.

## Health Concerns: What Science Currently Shows

Research on microplastic health effects is rapidly evolving. Here's what peer-reviewed studies have established so far:

**Documented concerns:**
- Nanoplastics can cross intestinal barriers, enter the bloodstream, and accumulate in organs including the liver, kidneys, and brain
- Plastic particles act as carriers for other toxic chemicals (pesticides, heavy metals, endocrine disruptors) that adsorb to their surfaces
- Laboratory studies show inflammatory responses in human cell lines exposed to microplastic concentrations
- Animal studies demonstrate reproductive effects, metabolic disruption, and neurotoxicity at relevant exposure levels

**Vulnerable populations:**
- **Infants and children:** Higher water intake per body weight means proportionally greater exposure; developing organs are more susceptible
- **Pregnant women:** Nanoplastics have been detected in placental tissue, raising concerns about fetal exposure
- **Immunocompromised individuals:** Inflammatory effects may be amplified

**The precautionary reality:** While definitive human health outcomes are still being studied, the combination of ubiquitous exposure, documented cellular-level effects, and the irreversibility of plastic accumulation in tissue makes precautionary reduction a scientifically reasonable approach.

## Which Filters Actually Remove Microplastics?

Not all filtration methods are equally effective against microplastics. Here's what the science shows:

### Reverse Osmosis (RO) — Most Effective (>99.9% removal)

RO membranes have pore sizes of 0.0001 microns — far smaller than even nanoplastics. They provide the most comprehensive removal of plastic particles of all sizes. Point-of-use RO systems under your kitchen sink are the gold standard for drinking water microplastic removal.

### Ultrafiltration (UF) — Highly Effective (>99% for microplastics, variable for nanoplastics)

UF membranes (0.01–0.1 micron pores) capture virtually all microplastics and most nanoplastics. Available as point-of-use or whole-home systems.

### Activated Carbon Block — Good (70–80% removal)

Dense carbon block filters (not granular activated carbon) physically trap many microplastics through adsorption and mechanical filtration. Effectiveness depends on block density and pore size rating. NSF/ANSI-certified carbon blocks with 0.5-micron ratings perform best.

### Granular Activated Carbon (GAC) — Limited (~40–60% removal)

Standard pitcher filters and refrigerator filters using loose granular carbon have inconsistent microplastic removal. Particles can pass through gaps between granules.

### Standard Sediment Filters — Minimal

Basic sediment filters rated at 5+ microns miss the vast majority of microplastics and essentially all nanoplastics.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does boiling water remove microplastics?

Recent research (2024) suggests that boiling hard water may encapsulate some microplastics in calcium carbonate crystite structures, which can then be filtered out. However, boiling alone doesn't eliminate nanoplastics from soft water, and it's not a practical daily solution. Point-of-use reverse osmosis remains the most reliable and consistent removal method.

### Are microplastics in tap water worse than bottled water?

The Columbia University study found bottled water contains approximately 240,000 particles per liter — potentially more than treated tap water. This is partly because plastic bottles themselves leach particles into the water, especially when exposed to heat or sunlight. Home-filtered tap water through an RO system provides dramatically lower microplastic exposure than either unfiltered tap or bottled water.

### How much microplastic do we consume annually?

Research estimates the average person ingests approximately 50,000 microplastic particles per year through drinking water alone, and up to 120,000 particles annually when food sources are included. A 2024 study estimated humans may accumulate a credit card's worth of plastic (about 5 grams) in their bodies every two weeks from all exposure routes combined.

### Will the EPA regulate microplastics in drinking water?

The CCL 6 listing is the first formal step, but establishing enforceable Maximum Contaminant Levels (MCLs) typically requires 10–20 years of additional research, rulemaking, and public comment periods. Based on historical precedent with other contaminants, federal microplastic drinking water standards are unlikely before 2035 at the earliest.
