## What Is Chloramine? (And Why Your Utility Chose It)

### The Chemistry

Chloramine is produced by combining chlorine (Cl₂) with ammonia (NH₃). The chemical reaction produces three possible compounds:

- **Monochloramine (NH₂Cl)** — the form used for water disinfection
- **Dichloramine (NHCl₂)** — forms in higher concentrations
- **Trichloramine (NCl₃)** — the strong-smelling compound you notice in indoor pools

For drinking water, utilities specifically produce monochloramine in concentrations of 1-4 mg/L (parts per million).

### Why Utilities Switch to Chloramine

Three factors drive the switch:

**1. Longer Persistence**
Chlorine dissipates quickly. In older, sprawling distribution systems, chlorine may be undetectable by the time water reaches the last customer. Chloramine persists 10-20 times longer, providing better coverage throughout extensive pipe networks.

**2. Lower Disinfection Byproducts (DBPs)**
Chlorine reacts with organic matter to form trihalomethanes (THMs) and haloacetic acids (HAA5s) — both regulated carcinogens. The EPA has progressively tightened limits on these DBPs. Chloramine produces significantly fewer of these specific compounds.

**3. Cost and Infrastructure**
Switching to chloramine often costs less than alternative solutions (granular activated carbon treatment at the utility level, or infrastructure upgrades). For budget-constrained utilities, chloramine provides regulatory compliance at lower cost.

## The Critical Difference: Why Chloramine Is Harder to Remove Than Chlorine

This is the point most homeowners completely miss — and why their existing filters fail:

**Chlorine removal is easy.** Standard activated carbon filters, Brita pitchers, faucet-mounted filters, shower filters, and basic whole-house systems all remove chlorine reasonably well. Even letting water sit out for 24 hours will dissipate most chlorine.

**Chloramine removal is HARD.** 

The ammonia-chlorine bond in chloramine is chemically stable. It doesn't break down in air. It doesn't evaporate easily. Standard activated carbon removes only 20-40% of chloramine through basic adsorption — leaving most of it in your water.

### What Actually Removes Chloramine

Three technologies effectively remove chloramine:

**1. Catalytic Carbon (Gold Standard)**
- Chemically modified carbon with enhanced surface catalytic properties
- Removes chloramine through catalytic reaction, not just adsorption
- 95-99% removal efficiency with proper contact time
- Used in quality whole-home filtration systems
- Requires extended contact time (slower flow rate)

**2. Ascorbic Acid (Vitamin C) Treatment**
- Chemically reduces/neutralizes chloramine
- Used in some drinking water filters and shower filters
- Limited capacity (needs frequent replacement)
- Effective but short-lived

**3. Reverse Osmosis**
- Removes chloramine along with everything else
- Typically combined with carbon pre-filter for membrane protection
- Point-of-use only (single tap)
- High waste water production

### What DOES NOT Remove Chloramine

❌ **Standard activated carbon filters** — Only 20-40% removal
❌ **Boiling water** — Chloramine is stable at boiling temperatures
❌ **Letting water sit out** — Chloramine persists for days
❌ **UV light alone** — Designed for bacteria, not chemical removal
❌ **Most pitcher filters** — Not designed for chloramine
❌ **Basic shower filters** — Almost none effectively remove chloramine
❌ **Sediment filters** — Physical filtration only

## Health Effects: Who Is Most Affected

### The General Population

For most healthy adults, chloramine in EPA-approved concentrations (under 4 mg/L) is considered safe to drink. However, research is ongoing, and chloramine can cause:

- Skin and eye irritation at higher concentrations
- Respiratory irritation from hot shower steam
- Digestive sensitivity in some individuals
- Taste and odor complaints

### Dialysis Patients (Critical Risk)

This is where chloramine becomes genuinely dangerous. Normal digestion processes chloramine safely, but when it enters the bloodstream directly during dialysis, it causes:

- **Hemolytic anemia** — destruction of red blood cells
- Oxidative damage
- Life-threatening complications

**All dialysis equipment in chloramine-treated areas MUST use carbon filtration and specific treatment protocols. Home hemodialysis users must verify their equipment's chloramine handling capability.**

If your city switches to chloramine and you or a family member receives dialysis, this must be communicated to your dialysis provider immediately.

### Aquarium and Pond Owners (Fish Die Without Action)

**This is the most immediate danger for affected households.**

Chlorine and chloramine are both toxic to fish. Chlorine dissipates if water sits 24+ hours. **Chloramine does not.** The ammonia component is especially toxic to fish and can rapidly kill aquarium populations.

**Required actions for aquarium/pond owners when city switches to chloramine:**

1. Stop using your previous water conditioning routine immediately
2. Purchase a chloramine-specific dechlorinator (removes both chlorine and ammonia)
3. Popular effective products: Seachem Prime, API Stress Coat+, Fluval Aqua Plus
4. Test water ammonia levels before adding fish — chloramine removal releases ammonia
5. Increase biological filtration to handle ammonia from chloramine breakdown
6. For ponds: calculate dosages carefully for large water volumes

Many beginner aquarists don't realize their utility switched and lose their entire aquarium within days of the first water change after the switch.

### Pregnant Women and Infants

Research on chloramine and pregnancy/infants is still developing, but precautionary guidance includes:

- Limit exposure to hot shower steam (inhalation route)
- Use filtered water for baby formula preparation
- Consider whole-home chloramine removal for sensitive pregnancies
- Consult your obstetrician about individual risk factors

### People with Skin Conditions

If you have eczema, psoriasis, or sensitive skin, chloramine exposure through bathing and showering can worsen symptoms:

- Chloramine damages skin barrier function similar to chlorine
- Dichloramine and trichloramine in hot steam cause additional irritation
- Many patients report symptom improvement after chloramine removal

Dermatologists increasingly recommend whole-home chloramine filtration for patients with chronic skin conditions.

### Pets (Especially Reptiles and Amphibians)

- Dogs and cats: Generally handle chloramine similarly to humans
- Reptiles and amphibians: More sensitive due to permeable skin
- Birds: Sensitive to respiratory effects from chloramine-containing steam
- Consider filtered water for pet drinking water, especially for exotic pets

## How to Know If Your Water Has Chloramine

### Method 1: Check Your Consumer Confidence Report

Your utility's annual CCR (available online or on request) lists the disinfectant used. Look for terms:
- "Chloramine" or "Chloramination" = Yes
- "Chlorine" or "Free Chlorine" = No (currently)
- "Combined Chlorine" = Yes (chloramine)
- Specific mention of "monochloramine" = Yes

### Method 2: Test Your Water

DIY test kits specifically for chloramine include:
- **Hach Chloramine Test Kit** — professional-grade, approximately $50
- **API Ammonia Test Kit** — detects the ammonia component, $10-15
- **Total Chlorine Test Strips** — measures free + combined chlorine, $10-15

A comparison of "free chlorine" vs "total chlorine" readings reveals chloramine presence. If total chlorine is significantly higher than free chlorine, you have chloramine.

### Method 3: Sensory Indicators

Chloramine has distinct characteristics:
- Milder smell than chlorine at low concentrations
- Persistent smell in hot water even after sitting
- Slightly sweet/ammonia-like taste in some cases
- "Indoor pool smell" in bathroom steam (indicates high trichloramine)

### Method 4: Call Your Utility

Simply call your water utility's customer service line and ask. They must tell you their disinfection method.

## Finding the Right Filter: Certifications to Demand

When shopping for a chloramine filter, NSF certifications matter. Look specifically for:

**NSF/ANSI Standard 42** — Aesthetic effects (chlorine, taste, odor)
- Make sure the product claim specifically includes "chloramine reduction"
- Standard 42 certification alone doesn't guarantee chloramine removal
- Product testing documentation should show specific chloramine performance

**NSF/ANSI Standard 401** — Emerging contaminants
- Additional certification for pharmaceutical and chemical removal

**Water Quality Association (WQA) Gold Seal** — Independent verification

Ask these questions before purchasing:
- Has this specific product been tested and certified for chloramine removal?
- What is the claimed removal percentage? (Should be 95%+ for quality systems)
- How long is the media life for my water usage and chloramine level?
- What is the required flow rate / contact time?
- What is the whole-system pressure drop?

Beware of products marketed as "removes chlorine and chloramine" without certification documentation. Many don't actually remove chloramine effectively.

## FAQs

**Q: Can a regular water filter remove chloramine?**
A: Standard activated carbon filters CANNOT effectively remove chloramine — only 20-40% removal. Chloramine requires catalytic carbon filtration with extended contact time, or reverse osmosis. Look for NSF/ANSI Standard 42 certified systems specifically claiming chloramine reduction.

**Q: Why is my city switching from chlorine to chloramine?**
A: Chloramine persists 10-20x longer in the distribution system, ensuring disinfection reaches the end of extensive pipe networks. It also produces fewer regulated disinfection byproducts (THMs, HAA5s) than chlorine. About 1 in 5 US utilities have made this switch.

**Q: Does boiling water remove chloramine?**
A: NO. Unlike chlorine which evaporates with boiling, chloramine is stable and boiling is NOT effective. Even boiling for extended periods removes less than 10% of chloramine.

**Q: Is chloramine dangerous to drink?**
A: For most healthy adults at EPA-approved levels (under 4 mg/L), chloramine is considered safe to drink. However, it can be harmful for: dialysis patients (life-threatening), aquarium fish (rapidly fatal), and may aggravate skin conditions in sensitive individuals.

**Q: How long does it take for chloramine to dissipate?**
A: Unlike chlorine (24-48 hours), chloramine can persist in water for WEEKS without active removal. Letting water sit out is NOT an effective chloramine removal method.

**Q: Will a shower filter remove chloramine?**
A: Most standard shower filters are ineffective against chloramine. A few specialized chloramine-certified shower filters exist (using vitamin C / ascorbic acid), but they're short-lived (often 1-2 months). Whole-home catalytic carbon systems provide more reliable protection.

**Q: My fish died after our city switched — what happened?**
A: Almost certainly chloramine exposure. The ammonia component of chloramine is highly toxic to fish, and standard "chlorine neutralizer" products don't remove it. You need a chloramine-specific conditioner (like Seachem Prime) for all water changes.
