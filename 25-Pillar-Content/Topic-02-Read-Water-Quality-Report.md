## How to Read Your Water Quality Report (CCR): The 5 Numbers That Actually Matter

### What Is a Consumer Confidence Report?

Every July, your water utility is legally required to send you a document called a Consumer Confidence Report (CCR), also known as an Annual Water Quality Report. It summarizes what's in your tap water — the contaminants detected, their levels, and whether they meet federal standards.

Most people throw it away. That's a mistake.

This single document tells you more about your family's daily chemical exposure than any other source. But it's written in technical jargon that makes it nearly impossible for normal humans to interpret. This guide translates the report into plain English and shows you exactly which numbers matter.

### How to Find Your CCR

- **Online:** Search "[your city name] water quality report" or "[utility name] CCR"
- **EPA database:** Visit epa.gov/ccr and search by state and utility
- **Phone:** Call your utility's customer service number and request a copy
- **Mail:** Most utilities mail hard copies annually (usually with your water bill)

If you're on a private well, you won't have a CCR — you're responsible for your own testing.

### The 5 Numbers You Must Check

#### Number 1: Disinfection Byproducts (TTHMs and HAA5s)

**What they are:** When chlorine reacts with natural organic matter in water, it creates trihalomethanes (TTHMs) and haloacetic acids (HAA5s). Both are linked to increased cancer risk.

**EPA limit:** 80 ppb for TTHMs, 60 ppb for HAA5s

**What to look for:** If your utility reports TTHMs above 40 ppb or HAA5s above 30 ppb, your water has significant disinfection byproduct levels — even though it's "in compliance." A quality carbon filter removes these effectively.

**Your action threshold:** Above 50% of the maximum = worth filtering

#### Number 2: Lead

**What it is:** Toxic heavy metal from old pipes and solder. No safe level exists for children.

**EPA action level:** 15 ppb (measured at the 90th percentile — meaning 10% of homes tested exceeded this)

**What to look for:** Any detectable lead warrants attention. Above 5 ppb is concerning for households with children. The CCR shows the utility's system-wide testing — YOUR home's actual level could be higher depending on your plumbing age.

**Your action threshold:** Any detection above 1 ppb = consider filtration, especially with children in the home

#### Number 3: Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) and Hardness

**What they are:** TDS measures everything dissolved in your water. Hardness specifically measures calcium and magnesium.

**EPA secondary standard:** 500 ppm TDS (aesthetic, not enforced)

**What to look for:** Hardness above 120 mg/L (approximately 7 grains per gallon) means your water is "hard" and damaging appliances, drying skin, and creating scale.

**Your action threshold:** Above 7 GPG = water softener recommended

#### Number 4: Chlorine/Chloramine Residual

**What it is:** The disinfectant your utility adds to kill bacteria. Safe but causes taste, odor, and skin effects.

**EPA limit:** 4 mg/L (chlorine or chloramine)

**What to look for:** Most utilities maintain 0.5-2.0 mg/L. Above 1.0 mg/L causes noticeable taste/smell for most people. Note whether your utility uses chlorine OR chloramine — this determines which filter technology you need.

**Your action threshold:** Above 1.0 mg/L AND you notice taste/smell = filtration improves quality of life significantly

#### Number 5: Violation History

**What it is:** The CCR must disclose any violations during the reporting period — times when contaminants exceeded EPA limits.

**What to look for:** ANY health-based violation is serious. Even "monitoring violations" (failure to test on schedule) suggest oversight problems. Check the violation type:
- **MCL violation:** Contaminant exceeded maximum allowed level (health concern)
- **Treatment technique violation:** Required treatment wasn't performed properly
- **Monitoring violation:** Testing wasn't done on schedule (what are they hiding?)

**Your action threshold:** Any MCL violation = immediate filtration recommended

### What Your CCR Doesn't Tell You

Your CCR has significant limitations:

1. **It doesn't test at your tap.** Results reflect the treatment plant or distribution system — not what comes out of your specific faucet after passing through your home's plumbing.

2. **It doesn't test for everything.** Only EPA-regulated contaminants are reported. PFAS testing only became mandatory recently. Pharmaceuticals, microplastics, and many industrial chemicals aren't included.

3. **It uses annual averages.** A single high reading can be diluted by months of normal readings. "Average compliance" can mask periodic spikes.

4. **It doesn't account for your plumbing.** If your home has lead pipes, copper corrosion, or aging galvanized steel — contamination happens AFTER the water leaves the utility's system.

5. **Results are 6-18 months old.** The CCR published in July 2026 reflects testing from January-December 2025.

### How to Use Your CCR Results

**If all 5 numbers look good:** Your utility is providing adequate baseline water quality. You may still benefit from filtration for taste improvement or additional protection, but it's not urgent.

**If 1-2 numbers concern you:** Targeted filtration addresses your specific issues. For example: high chlorine → carbon filter. High hardness → softener.

**If 3+ numbers concern you:** Comprehensive whole-home treatment is warranted. An all-in-one system addresses multiple issues simultaneously.

**If violations are reported:** Don't wait. Install filtration immediately and monitor your utility's remediation progress.

## FAQs

**Q: Where do I find my water quality report?**
A: Search online for "[your city] water quality report" or visit epa.gov/ccr. Your utility must provide it free of charge annually, usually available online as a PDF.

**Q: What if my CCR shows everything "in compliance"?**
A: "In compliance" means below EPA maximums — but many contaminants exist at levels between zero and the maximum that still warrant attention. Additionally, your CCR doesn't test at your specific tap or cover unregulated contaminants.

**Q: My CCR shows chloramine instead of chlorine. What does that mean?**
A: Chloramine is harder to remove than chlorine. Standard carbon filters (Brita, basic faucet filters) are ineffective against chloramine. You need catalytic carbon filtration specifically designed for chloramine removal.

**Q: How often is my water tested by the utility?**
A: Frequency varies by contaminant. Bacteria: daily to weekly. Lead: every 1-3 years. Chemical contaminants: quarterly to annually. This means between tests, quality can change without detection.
