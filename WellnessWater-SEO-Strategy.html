What's Actually in Your Home's Water?

Before you can choose the right filtration system, you need to understand what you're filtering out. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) regulates over 90 contaminants in drinking water — but thousands more exist in measurable quantities in American tap water that are either unregulated or subject to outdated standards.

The 7 Most Common Contaminants in U.S. Tap Water

1. Chlorine & Chloramines

Used by 98% of U.S. municipal water systems as disinfectants. While they kill pathogens, they also produce disinfection byproducts (DBPs) like trihalomethanes, which have been linked to increased cancer risk. Chloramines are particularly difficult to remove — standard carbon filters won't cut it.

2. Lead

Primarily leached from old plumbing (particularly in homes built before 1986). There is no safe level of lead exposure, especially for children — even low levels cause permanent cognitive damage. An estimated 9.2 million U.S. homes still have lead service lines.

3. PFAS (Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances)

The "forever chemicals" found in 45% of U.S. drinking water samples, according to the USGS. Linked to kidney cancer, thyroid disease, developmental issues, and immune system suppression. The EPA set new enforceable limits in 2024, but most municipal systems won't be compliant for years.

4. Hard Water Minerals (Calcium & Magnesium)

Not harmful to drink, but devastating to your home's plumbing, appliances, and your skin/hair. Hard water costs the average American household $800 to $1,500 per year in premature appliance failure and cleaning products.

5. Heavy Metals (Arsenic, Cadmium, Mercury, Copper)

Present in both municipal and well water sources. Often undetectable without professional testing. Long-term exposure linked to cancers, kidney damage, and neurological conditions.

6. Pesticides, Herbicides & Pharmaceutical Residues

Agricultural runoff and improper disposal have introduced trace amounts of atrazine, glyphosate, antibiotics, antidepressants, and birth control hormones into public water supplies. Municipal treatment plants are not designed to remove these.

7. Microplastics

Studies have found microplastics in 94% of U.S. tap water samples. Health implications are still being studied, but early research suggests endocrine disruption and inflammatory responses.

Reality Check: The EPA does not require municipal utilities to test for or remove most of these contaminants. Your water utility can be 100% "in compliance" while delivering water containing dozens of harmful substances. The only way to know what's actually in your water is professional testing — and the only way to consistently remove these contaminants is a quality whole house filtration system.

The 6 Types of Whole House Water Filtration Technology

1. Sediment Pre-Filters

What it removes: Sand, silt, rust, dirt, visible particles
Technology: Mechanical filtration through graduated density media
Best for: Well water users, older homes with corroding pipes, as a first-stage filter
What it won't do: Remove dissolved chemicals, chlorine, or hardness minerals
Typical cost: $80 – $300

2. Activated Carbon Filtration

What it removes: Chlorine, taste/odor, some VOCs, pesticides
Technology: Adsorption through coconut shell or bituminous carbon
Best for: Municipal water users concerned with chlorine taste and smell
What it won't do: Remove minerals, heavy metals effectively, or chloramines (requires catalytic carbon)
Typical cost: $400 – $1,500

3. Catalytic Carbon Filtration

What it removes: Everything standard carbon removes PLUS chloramines, hydrogen sulfide, and certain pharmaceutical residues
Technology: Chemically modified carbon with enhanced catalytic reactions
Best for: Municipalities using chloramines (increasingly common), homes with "egg smell" water
Typical cost: $800 – $2,500

4. Water Softening (Ion Exchange)

What it removes: Calcium and magnesium (hardness minerals) via sodium/potassium exchange
Technology: Resin beads that trade hardness ions for sodium ions
Best for: Homes in hard water regions (most of the southwestern, midwestern, and Texas US)
Drawbacks: Adds sodium to water, requires salt refills, discharges brine
Typical cost: $500 – $2,500

5. Salt-Free Water Conditioning

What it does: Prevents scale formation by altering mineral crystal structure (TAC - Template Assisted Crystallization)
Technology: Catalytic media that changes mineral behavior without removing them
Best for: Environmentally-conscious homeowners, low-sodium diets, areas with salt-discharge restrictions
Note: Minerals remain in the water (some see this as a benefit)
Typical cost: $1,000 – $3,000

6. All-in-One Mineralized & Alkaline Filtration

What it does: Removes contaminants + softens water + re-adds beneficial minerals + alkalizes pH
Technology: Multi-stage proprietary media combining catalytic carbon, softening, and remineralization
Best for: Health-focused homeowners wanting comprehensive water wellness in a single system
Advantages: Single installation, complete solution, added health benefits of mineralized alkaline water at every tap
Typical cost: $4,000 – $8,000

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a whole house water filtration system cost?
Whole house systems range from $800 for basic setups to $8,000+ for premium all-in-one systems. Most quality systems fall in the $3,500 to $7,500 range installed. The true value comes from long-term savings on bottled water, appliance longevity, and reduced plumbing repairs.
How long do whole house water filters last?
Quality systems last 10 to 15 years. Filter cartridges are typically replaced every 6 to 12 months, while primary media tanks last 5 to 10 years. Premium mineralized systems can last 15+ years with proper maintenance.
Do whole house water filters reduce water pressure?
A properly-sized professional system will cause minimal pressure loss (typically 2 to 5 PSI). Undersized systems or poor installations can reduce pressure significantly. Always work with certified installers who match system capacity to your household needs.
Can whole house filters remove PFAS and lead?
Yes — but only advanced systems with multi-stage catalytic carbon and specialized media. Basic sediment filters will not remove these contaminants. Look for NSF/ANSI 53 and NSF/ANSI 401 certifications to verify performance against these contaminants.
What's the difference between a water softener and a water filter?
A softener removes hardness minerals (calcium/magnesium) to prevent scale. A filter removes contaminants like chlorine, lead, and chemicals. Most homes need both — which is why all-in-one systems like Wellness Water are increasingly popular.
Do I need a water softener if I have city water?
Depends on your municipal source. Cities like Phoenix, Las Vegas, Dallas, and much of the Southwest have very hard water despite being municipal. Your utility's annual water report will list hardness in grains per gallon (GPG). Above 7 GPG generally warrants softening.

What Is Alkaline Water? (The Real Definition)

The pH scale measures how acidic or alkaline (basic) a liquid is, on a scale of 0 to 14:

  • 0 to 6.9: Acidic (lemon juice = 2, coffee = 5)
  • 7.0: Neutral (pure distilled water)
  • 7.1 to 14: Alkaline (baking soda solution = 9, ammonia = 11)

Regular tap water typically has a pH between 6.5 and 8.5, depending on your source. Reverse osmosis (RO) water drops to around 5.0 to 6.5 because the filtration process strips minerals that buffer pH.

Alkaline water is generally defined as water with a pH of 8.0 or higher. However, the critical distinction most marketers hide is how that pH was achieved:

The 3 Types of Alkaline Water (Not All Are Equal)

Type 1: Naturally Mineralized Alkaline Water

Water that's alkaline because it contains naturally occurring minerals — calcium, magnesium, potassium, and bicarbonates picked up as water flows through limestone, dolomite, and volcanic rock. This is how premium spring waters from Fiji, Iceland, and the French Alps acquire their alkalinity.

Health relevance: HIGH — the minerals provide bioavailable nutrition PLUS buffer pH

Type 2: Artificially Ionized Alkaline Water

Water that's been run through an electrical ionizer that separates hydrogen and hydroxyl ions, increasing pH without adding minerals. Produced by countertop ionizer machines costing $1,500 to $5,000.

Health relevance: MIXED — pH is elevated but mineral content remains whatever was in the source water. Claims of "hydrogen-rich" water benefits are still being researched.

Type 3: pH-Adjusted Bottled Water

Water where alkalinity was achieved by adding baking soda, potassium hydroxide, or other alkalizing agents. Much of the bottled "alkaline water" in stores falls in this category.

Health relevance: LOW — no meaningful mineral content, just elevated pH. Often contains sodium.

The Critical Insight: When we talk about "alkaline water benefits," we're primarily talking about Type 1 — naturally mineralized alkaline water. This is what your body has evolved to process over millions of years. It's also what Wellness Water Company's mineralization system produces at every tap in your home.

Practical implication: For GERD sufferers, drinking alkaline water may reduce symptoms and tissue damage.

What the Research Does NOT Support

To be honest about the science, the following popular claims are not robustly supported by peer-reviewed research:

  • "Changing your body's pH" (your blood pH is tightly regulated by your body regardless of what you drink)
  • Significant weight loss benefits
  • Reversing aging
  • Eliminating chronic disease

These claims are either exaggerated marketing or extrapolations from lab studies that haven't been confirmed in human clinical trials. Legitimate benefits exist — but they're more modest than social media suggests.

Why Reverse Osmosis Water May Actually Harm You Long-Term

This is the part the RO system industry doesn't want you to know.

Reverse osmosis filtration removes contaminants by forcing water through a semi-permeable membrane that rejects virtually everything larger than a water molecule. It's highly effective at removing bad stuff — but it also removes all the good stuff: calcium, magnesium, potassium, sodium, bicarbonates, and trace minerals.

The WHO's Warning About Demineralized Water

In 2005, the World Health Organization published a report titled "Health Risks from Drinking Demineralised Water" that explicitly cautioned against long-term consumption of fully demineralized water. Key findings:

  • Demineralized water leaches minerals from the body as it's absorbed
  • Long-term consumption correlated with cardiovascular issues and neuromuscular symptoms
  • Cooking food in demineralized water causes significant mineral loss in food itself
  • Very low calcium and magnesium intake from water is linked to increased mortality

The WHO recommended that drinking water contain at least 10 mg/L of magnesium and 20 mg/L of calcium. Most RO water contains virtually zero.

RO water typically has a pH of 5.0 to 6.5 — mildly acidic. This creates two issues:

  1. Taste: Acidic water tastes flat, metallic, or "empty" to most palates
  2. Corrosion: Acidic water gradually corrodes copper plumbing, leaching copper into your water over years

What Is Hard Water? The Chemistry Explained

Hard water is water with high concentrations of dissolved minerals — primarily calcium (Ca²⁺) and magnesium (Mg²⁺). These minerals are picked up as groundwater flows through limestone, chalk, and gypsum deposits. The harder the geology your water passes through, the harder your water will be.

How Hard Water Damage Happens (Microscopic View)

When hard water is heated, the dissolved calcium and magnesium come out of solution and form limescale — a hard, crusty deposit. This happens:

  • In your water heater: Scale coats the heating element, reducing efficiency and eventually causing burnout
  • In your dishwasher: Scale builds up in the heating coil, spray arms, and water jets — reducing cleaning power
  • In your pipes: Scale gradually narrows the diameter, restricting flow and pressure
  • In your faucets: Scale clogs aerators and cartridges, causing drips and reduced flow
  • On your skin: Calcium combines with soap to form "soap scum" that clogs pores and dries skin
  • In your washing machine: Scale coats the drum, heating element, and hoses, degrading performance
