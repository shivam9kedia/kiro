## TARGET AUDIENCE SEGMENTS

### Segment 1 — The Concerned City-Water Family (High Priority)
Suburban homeowners on municipal water who saw a news story about PFAS, lead, or "forever chemicals."
- **Pain:** Chlorine taste/smell, fear of contaminants, kids' health, distrust of the utility report.
- **Content need:** Plain-English contaminant guides, "is my tap water safe," how to read a water-quality report, removal solutions.

### Segment 2 — The Private Well Owner (High Priority)
Rural/exurban homeowners with no municipal oversight, responsible for their own testing.
- **Pain:** Iron, sulfur smell, bacteria, nitrates, hardness, sediment; "no one is testing this for me."
- **Content need:** Well testing guides, contaminant-specific treatment, maintenance.

### Segment 3 — The Hard-Water Sufferer (High Priority)
Homeowners battling scale, spotty dishes, dry skin/hair, dying appliances.
- **Pain:** Limescale, appliance repair bills, dull hair, itchy skin, soap that won't lather.
- **Content need:** Hardness maps, true-cost calculators, softener vs. conditioner decisions, skin/hair relief.

### Segment 4 — The Health & Wellness Optimizer (Medium Priority)
Hydration-focused, clean-living buyers — often the household decision-maker.
- **Pain:** Wants the "cleanest" water for energy, skin, longevity, coffee, babies, pets.
- **Content need:** Health/lifestyle angles, bottled-vs-filtered, water for babies/pets/cooking.

### Segment 5 — The Researching Buyer (High Commercial Value)
Ready to invest but comparing options, costs, and companies.
- **Pain:** "How much does this cost? RO or whole-house? Which company can I trust?"
- **Content need:** Pricing/ROI, buyer's checklists, certifications, installation expectations, comparisons.

# MASTER ON-PAGE & TECHNICAL SEO CHECKLIST
*Apply to every one of the 25 pillars. This is the "minor to major" element list that turns a good article into a ranking, converting asset.*

## 1. Keyword & Intent Architecture
- One **primary focus keyword** per pillar in: H1, first 100 words, one H2, meta title, URL, image alt, and conclusion.
- **Secondary + LSI/semantic keywords** woven naturally (no stuffing) — target ~0.8–1.5% density for the focus term.
- Map each pillar to a single **search intent**; don't mix transactional and informational angles on one URL.
- Build a **"People Also Ask" capture layer**: phrase at least 4–6 H2/H3s as natural questions.

## 2. Title & Meta
- **Meta title** ≤ 60 characters, focus keyword near the front, brand or year where space allows.
- **Meta description** 140–160 characters, includes focus keyword + a benefit + a soft CTA.
- One **H1 only**, distinct from the meta title (reduces over-optimization, captures more variants).

## 3. URL & Structure
- Short, lowercase, hyphenated slugs containing the focus keyword (no dates, no stop-word clutter).
- Logical heading hierarchy (H1 → H2 → H3); never skip levels.

# PFAS in Your Drinking Water: What "Forever Chemicals" Are — and How to Get Them Out

You filled a glass from the kitchen tap this morning without a second thought. Most of us do. But over the last few years, a quiet phrase has crept from scientific journals into the evening news and onto the side of your water utility's annual report: **PFAS — "forever chemicals."**

If you've felt a flicker of unease about what's actually in that glass, you're not overreacting. PFAS are now detectable in the drinking water of an estimated majority of Americans, in both city supplies and private wells. The good news, and the reason this guide exists, is simple: **PFAS can be removed from your water — and you don't have to guess about whether they're there.**

> **Key takeaways**
> - PFAS are a family of thousands of synthetic chemicals that don't break down naturally — hence "forever."
> - They reach your tap from industrial sites, firefighting foam, landfills, and everyday products.

---

## What Are PFAS (and Why "Forever")?

PFAS stands for **per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances** — a family of more than 12,000 man-made chemicals first engineered in the 1940s. You've used them your whole life without knowing it: nonstick pans, waterproof jackets, grease-resistant food wrappers, stain-proof carpet, and firefighting foam all rely on them.

What makes PFAS so useful is also what makes them a problem. The bond between carbon and fluorine atoms is one of the strongest in chemistry. It resists heat, water, and oil — and it resists breaking down in nature. While a fallen leaf decomposes in a season, PFAS can persist in soil and water for decades or longer. They don't biodegrade. They just move. That's the "forever" in forever chemicals.

The two most studied compounds are **PFOA** and **PFOS**. Both were largely phased out of US manufacturing, but because they don't break down, they're still in the environment — and in us.

---

## How PFAS Get Into Your Tap and Well Water

PFAS rarely start in your water. They start somewhere upstream and travel.

- **Industrial discharge:** Factories that made or used PFAS released them into the air and water for decades.
- **Firefighting foam (AFFF):** Used heavily at military bases and airports, it soaked into groundwater across the country.
- **Landfills:** Every PFAS-treated product we throw away can leach chemicals into the ground.
- **Wastewater and biosolids:** Treated sludge spread on farmland can carry PFAS into nearby water sources.

From there, gravity and groundwater do the rest. The chemicals migrate into aquifers and reservoirs — the same sources that feed both **municipal systems and private wells**. If you're on a well near any of those sources, you're especially exposed, because no utility is testing your water for you. (If you're a well owner, our [Well Water Testing Guide](#) walks through exactly what to check.)

## The Health Concerns Behind the Headlines

> **Medically reviewed by Dr. Ronald Sidney Stuart, M.D.**
> *"The thing patients find unsettling about PFAS is that you can't see, smell, or taste them — and the exposure is chronic, not a single bad glass. That's exactly why filtration at the source is such a reasonable, proactive step for a family."*

Research into PFAS is ongoing, and responsible sources are careful not to overstate what's proven. Here's the balanced picture: major health agencies, including the CDC's ATSDR, have linked higher PFAS exposure to a range of potential effects studied in populations — among them changes in cholesterol, effects on the immune response, and concerns for development during pregnancy and infancy.

The key word researchers keep returning to is **cumulative**. The concern isn't one glass of water; it's small amounts, every day, for years, building up in the body. That's why reducing ongoing exposure — rather than panicking about a single drink — is the sensible approach.

## The 2026 EPA PFAS Limits, Explained Simply

For most of PFAS history, there was no enforceable national limit for them in drinking water. That changed.

In **2024, the EPA finalized the first-ever national drinking water standards for PFAS**, setting enforceable limits for several compounds — including PFOA and PFOS — at just **4 parts per trillion**. To picture how small that is: one part per trillion is roughly a single drop in 20 Olympic-sized swimming pools. The fact that regulators chose a number that low tells you how seriously the science is being taken.

Utilities now have a multi-year window to test, monitor, and reduce PFAS to meet these limits. That's progress — but "your utility is working on it" and "your water is clean today" are not the same sentence. Which raises the only question that really matters for your household.
