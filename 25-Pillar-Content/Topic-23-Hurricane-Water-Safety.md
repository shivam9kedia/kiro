## Hurricane and Flood Season: How to Protect Your Home's Water Supply Before, During, and After a Storm

When a major hurricane makes landfall, municipal water treatment plants may lose power, well pumps stop working, and floodwaters introduce dangerous contamination into drinking water sources. FEMA reports that 40% of small community water systems experience service disruption during major hurricanes, and private well owners face even greater vulnerability with no utility backup system protecting their supply.

The difference between a manageable inconvenience and a genuine health crisis often comes down to preparation completed days or weeks before the storm arrives—not in the frantic hours before landfall. This guide covers the complete timeline of water safety for hurricane-prone homeowners.

---

### Before the Storm: 72-Hour Preparation Window

The moment a tropical system enters a potential threat zone (typically 5-7 days out), begin water preparations. Don't wait for mandatory evacuation orders.

**Water storage essentials:**

- **Minimum need:** 1 gallon per person per day for 7 days (drinking and sanitation)
- **Realistic target:** 2 gallons per person per day for 7-14 days (accounts for cooking, cleaning, pets, and extended outages)
- **Family of four, two weeks:** Approximately 110 gallons minimum

**Storage methods (ranked by reliability):**
1. **Commercially sealed water** — Indefinite shelf life when sealed; store in cool dark location
2. **Food-grade containers** — Fill with tap water 24-48 hours before landfall; add 4 drops unscented bleach per gallon if storing more than 72 hours
3. **Bathtub storage** — Fill bathtubs with clean water for non-drinking use (flushing toilets, washing). Use a WaterBOB or similar liner for drinking-quality bathtub storage
4. **Water heater tank** — Your 40-80 gallon tank is an emergency water reservoir (turn off heating element and inlet valve to preserve)

**Document your water treatment system:**
- Photograph all equipment serial numbers and model numbers
- Record your well depth, pump capacity, and pressure settings
- Note filter replacement dates and media types
- Save installer contact information in your phone AND on paper
- Know your equipment's electrical requirements for generator sizing

**Protect water treatment equipment:**
- Turn off power to UV systems, water softeners, and booster pumps
- Close well cap vents or cover with waterproof tape if flooding is expected
- Secure any outdoor water treatment components against wind
- Disconnect electrical connections to well pumps if flooding will reach the wellhead

### During the Storm: Protocols for Active Hurricane Conditions

Once the storm arrives, your focus shifts to preservation and contamination prevention:

**Well pump protocol:**
- **If you have a generator:** Run the well pump briefly to confirm operation, then store water while power lasts. Don't run continuously—preserve fuel for extended outage
- **If power is out without backup:** Your pressure tank holds 10-30 gallons of pressurized water depending on size. Use sparingly until power returns
- **If flooding reaches your well:** STOP USING IMMEDIATELY. Assume contamination until professional testing confirms safety

**Municipal water users:**
- Fill remaining containers immediately if you haven't already—pressure may drop or boil orders may be issued
- If a boil water advisory is issued, boil for a full rolling minute (3 minutes above 6,500 feet elevation)
- Do not use tap water for any purpose if a "do not use" order is issued (more severe than boil order)

**Generator considerations for water treatment:**
- UV disinfection systems: 40-80 watts (minimal generator load)
- Well pump: 750-1,500 watts starting, 500-1,000 watts running
- Water softener: 20-50 watts during regeneration only
- Booster pump: 500-750 watts
- RO system: Negligible draw (pressure-driven)

Prioritize generator power for the well pump over treatment systems. Untreated water from your well is almost certainly safer than no water at all—you can boil for bacterial safety while conserving treatment system electronics for post-storm use.

### After the Storm: The Critical First 72 Hours

The most dangerous period for water contamination is immediately after a storm, when homeowners are eager to return to normalcy but contamination risk is at its peak.

**For well water owners — DO NOT USE YOUR WELL until:**

1. **Visual inspection complete:** Check wellhead for damage, flooding evidence, or debris. If any surface water contacted the well casing, assume contamination.
2. **Electrical safety confirmed:** Have an electrician verify well pump wiring if any flooding occurred near electrical components.
3. **Water tested for bacteria:** Total coliform and E. coli testing is non-negotiable after any flooding event. Results typically take 24-48 hours from a certified lab.
4. **Appearance normalized:** Run water from an outdoor spigot (not through your treatment system) until it runs clear. Cloudy, discolored, or odorous water indicates contamination.

**Signs of flood contamination in your well:**
- Water is muddy, cloudy, or has unusual color
- Unusual taste or odor (chemical, sewage, petroleum)
- Debris visible around wellhead or in well cap
- Standing water near or above the well casing during storm
- Nearby septic systems flooded or damaged
- Chemical storage (fertilizers, fuel) flooded upstream of well

**When to shock chlorinate your well:**

Shock chlorination (super-chlorination) is recommended when:
- Any bacterial test returns positive after a storm
- Floodwaters contacted the wellhead
- You observe any visual contamination indicators
- Your well was submerged during the storm

**Shock chlorination procedure:**
1. Calculate well volume (depth × casing diameter conversion)
2. Add household bleach (sodium hypochlorite) to achieve 200 ppm throughout the well
3. Circulate by running each faucet until chlorine smell is evident
4. Let stand 12-24 hours without water use
5. Flush system until chlorine is undetectable
6. Retest for bacteria 48 hours after flushing

**For municipal water users post-storm:**
- Follow ALL utility advisories—boil orders exist for your safety
- Don't assume water is safe when pressure returns; contamination can persist after service restoration
- Run cold water for 5 minutes at each tap before first use after extended outage
- Replace refrigerator water filters that sat in stagnant conditions for extended periods

### Water Treatment Systems as Permanent Emergency Preparedness

The most effective hurricane water strategy isn't last-minute preparation—it's a year-round water treatment system that doubles as emergency infrastructure:

**How existing treatment systems help during emergencies:**

- **Reverse osmosis systems:** Remove bacterial contamination, chemicals, and sediment from compromised water (provided pressure exists)
- **UV disinfection:** Eliminates 99.99% of bacteria and viruses when power is available (pair with generator for continuous protection)
- **Sediment filtration:** Prevents post-storm particulates from reaching your household plumbing
- **Storage tanks:** Treatment system storage tanks (RO tanks, pressure tanks) provide immediate emergency water reserves

**System investments that provide dual value:**
- A well pump with a dedicated generator connection serves daily and emergency needs
- A UV system protects against routine and post-storm bacterial risks
- A whole-house sediment filter protects plumbing from post-storm turbidity AND extends the life of downstream equipment year-round

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long can I store tap water for hurricane season?
Tap water stored in clean, food-grade containers with tight lids remains safe for approximately 6 months when stored in cool, dark conditions. Adding 4 drops of unscented liquid bleach (8.25% sodium hypochlorite) per gallon extends safe storage. Commercially bottled water has an indefinite shelf life when the seal is intact, though manufacturers typically print 1-2 year best-by dates for quality.

### Should I turn off my water softener before a hurricane?
Yes. Put your softener in bypass mode before the storm. This prevents contaminated water from flowing through and fouling the resin bed, prevents salt waste during a power outage (some units attempt regeneration on power-up), and makes post-storm flushing more effective since you're flushing the shortest possible pipe run.

### Can I use my pool water for drinking in an emergency?
Pool water can be used as a last-resort emergency drinking source if treated properly. The chlorine already present may not be at safe drinking levels, and pool water contains cyanuric acid, algaecides, and other chemicals not intended for consumption. If absolutely necessary, filter through clean cloth, boil for one minute, and use only short-term until better sources are available. Never drink pool water if it has been contaminated by floodwater.

### How do I know if my well pump survived the hurricane?
After power returns, listen for normal pump startup when you open a faucet. Warning signs include: no water flow at all (possible electrical damage or seized pump), cycling on/off rapidly (waterlogged pressure tank or lost prime), sand or debris in water (possible well screen damage), or unusual motor sounds (bearing damage from debris). If any of these occur, contact a well professional before continued operation to prevent further damage.
