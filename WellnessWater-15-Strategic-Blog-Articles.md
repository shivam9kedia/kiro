## Where Does LA's Tap Water Even Come From?

This is the part most people never learn. Los Angeles doesn't get most of its water locally — it **imports the majority of it from hundreds of miles away.** The Los Angeles Department of Water and Power (LADWP) sources a large share through the [Metropolitan Water District](https://www.ladwp.com/who-we-are/water-system/sources-supply/metropolitan-water-district-southern-california), which pulls from the **Colorado River** (via the 242-mile Colorado River Aqueduct) and Northern California (the State Water Project), plus the Los Angeles Aqueduct and some local groundwater and recycled water.

Why does that matter to your glass? Because **Colorado River water is naturally hard** — loaded with the calcium and magnesium it picks up traveling through mineral-rich terrain. That single fact explains a lot about life in LA.

## So, Is It Safe to Drink?

Yes — in the regulatory sense. LADWP's water consistently **meets or exceeds federal and California drinking water standards**, and California has some of the strictest water rules in the nation. For a healthy adult, LA tap water is considered safe to drink.

But "meets standards" answers a narrower question than most people are really asking. Legal limits allow measurable amounts of various substances, some standards lag the latest science, and — critically — **the utility's testing reflects the water leaving the system, not the water after it travels through your building's pipes.** That gap is where a lot of LA's real-world water issues live.

## What's Actually in LA Tap Water?

Here's a clear-eyed look at the things Angelenos most commonly notice or worry about:

| What | Why it's there | What it means for you |

| **Lead (possible at the tap)** | Older home plumbing/fixtures | Not from the source — from your building's pipes |

The big everyday one is **hardness**. The health-headline ones are chromium-6 and lead — chromium-6 because California made news regulating it, and lead because it can enter from old plumbing regardless of how clean the source water is.

## Why LA Water Feels So "Hard"

If your soap won't lather, your glasses come out cloudy, and there's white crust on every faucet, that's the imported mineral content at work. Hard water isn't a health hazard, but in LA it's a daily quality-of-life and home-maintenance issue — and it's the number-one reason Angelenos install water softeners or conditioners.

- **For older homes/apartments worried about lead:** test first, then use RO or an NSF-53-certified filter at the tap.

Most LA homes benefit from softening plus a drinking-water RO — the combination that tackles both the hardness and the contaminant concerns.

If you've seen the movie *Erin Brockovich*, you already know chromium-6 by reputation — the cancer-causing chemical at the heart of a real California town's water crisis. What you may not know is that in 2024, California did something the rest of the country still hasn't: it set an actual legal limit for it in drinking water. Here's what that means for you.

## Key Takeaways
- Chromium-6 (hexavalent chromium) is a recognized carcinogen, famous from the Hinkley, CA case.
- In 2024, California became the first state to set a chromium-6 drinking water limit: **10 ppb**.
- It enters water from industrial pollution and, in places, naturally from rock.

- **Reverse osmosis** is among the most effective home removal methods.

## What Is Chromium-6?

Chromium comes in different forms. Chromium-3 is a harmless nutrient your body needs in trace amounts. **Chromium-6 (hexavalent chromium) is the dangerous one** — a toxic, carcinogenic form used in industrial processes like metal plating, manufacturing, and cooling systems. It can also occur naturally in some groundwater as it interacts with certain rock formations.

The chemical entered the national consciousness through the case dramatized in *Erin Brockovich*, when the town of Hinkley, California suffered serious health impacts from chromium-6 contamination linked to a utility's operations. It put a name and a face to an invisible threat.

## The Health Concern

Chromium-6 is classified as a carcinogen, and long-term exposure through drinking water is associated with increased cancer risk. California's regulators estimated that even at the new 10 ppb limit, decades of daily exposure carries a measurable (if small) cancer risk — which is precisely why they pushed to regulate it. As with most contaminants, the concern is **chronic, cumulative exposure** rather than a single glass.

## The 2024 California Limit — A National First

For years there was no enforceable national or state limit specifically for chromium-6. That changed in California. On October 1, 2024, the [State Water Resources Control Board adopted a maximum contaminant level (MCL) of 10 parts per billion](https://www.waterboards.ca.gov/drinking_water/certlic/drinkingwater/Chromium6.shtml) for hexavalent chromium — the first such standard in the United States. Water systems must test for it and bring levels into compliance.

It's a landmark step. But two things are worth understanding: first, compliance rolls out over time, so detectable chromium-6 may still be present in some supplies during the transition; and second, **a limit of 10 ppb is a legal threshold, not a guarantee of zero.** If you want chromium-6 minimized in your drinking water, treating it at home is the surest route.

## How to Remove Chromium-6 From Your Water

Because chromium-6 is invisible and tasteless, **step one is testing** — you can't manage what you can't measure. If it's present, the most effective home solutions are:
- **Reverse osmosis** — the gold standard for chromium-6 reduction at the drinking tap, and it removes a long list of other contaminants too.
- **Specialty ion-exchange media** designed for chromium reduction (often used in larger or whole-home applications).

A standard carbon pitcher or fridge filter generally won't remove chromium-6 — this is a job for RO or dedicated media.

**Is chromium-6 dangerous in drinking water?**

**How do I remove chromium-6 from my water?**
Reverse osmosis is among the most effective home methods. Specialty ion-exchange media also work. Standard carbon filters generally do not remove it.

**How do I know if my water has chromium-6?**
It's invisible and tasteless, so testing is the only way. Check your water report and confirm with a lab or professional water test.

The distinction matters, because the actual amounts are smaller than the word "salt" makes people imagine.

## How Much Sodium Does Softening Actually Add?

Here's the part that calms most fears: the amount of sodium added is **directly proportional to how hard your water was.** The more hardness minerals removed, the more sodium added in exchange — but for typical household hardness, the increase per glass is modest. To put it in perspective, the sodium in a glass of softened water is generally small compared to what you get from everyday foods — a slice of bread, a serving of cheese, or a bowl of soup typically contains far more sodium than a glass of softened water.

For a healthy person with no sodium restriction, the added sodium from softened water is usually nutritionally minor.

## Why Flow Rate Is the Spec That Matters

That simultaneous demand is your **peak flow rate**, measured in **gallons per minute (GPM)**. If your filter's service flow rate is lower than your peak demand, the filter becomes a bottleneck and your pressure drops. Get the GPM right and the filter disappears into the background.

This is exactly why a generic "good for up to X people" label can mislead — what matters is GPM versus your peak demand.

## Step 1: Estimate Your Home's Peak Flow Rate

The most practical shortcut is to estimate by **number of bathrooms** (a strong proxy for fixture count and simultaneous use):

| Home size | Typical peak demand |
|-----------|--------------------|
| 1–2 bathrooms | ~5–10 GPM |
| 3–4 bathrooms | ~8–15 GPM |
| 5+ bathrooms / large household | ~15–25+ GPM |

For a more precise number, add up the flow rates of fixtures likely to run simultaneously (a shower is ~2–2.5 GPM, a dishwasher ~1–2 GPM, etc.) to estimate realistic peak use. Bigger families and homes with high simultaneous use should size toward the higher end.

## Step 2: Choose a Filter Rated At or Above Your Peak GPM

Now match the equipment. Select a whole-house filter whose **service flow rate meets or exceeds** your estimated peak demand. It's wise to build in a little headroom so the system isn't constantly running at its maximum. A filter comfortably rated above your peak demand maintains strong pressure and won't choke during busy mornings.

## Step 3: Don't Forget Port Size and Media Capacity

Two more factors that quietly affect flow:
- **Port/pipe size:** The filter's inlet/outlet should match your main line (commonly ¾-inch or 1-inch). An undersized port restricts flow even if the GPM rating looks fine. Larger homes often need 1-inch ports.
- **Media/tank capacity:** Beyond instantaneous flow, the system's capacity affects how long media lasts before service. Higher-demand homes benefit from larger tanks.

## Step 4: Account for Your Water Quality

Sizing isn't only about volume — it's about your water. Very hard water, high sediment, or heavy contaminant loads can mean you need a larger system, a sediment pre-filter, or a multi-stage train so one stage doesn't get overwhelmed. This is where a water test feeds directly into correct sizing.

Skip it, and you risk the weak-shower regret that makes people blame "the filter."

"What size water softener do I need?" is one of the smartest questions a buyer can ask — and one of the most poorly answered online, where you'll find vague "get a 40,000-grain unit" advice with no reasoning. The truth is, the right size depends on a simple formula using three numbers about your home. Let's actually do the math.

## Key Takeaways
- Water softeners are sized by **grain capacity** — how much hardness they remove before regenerating.
- The formula: **people × daily water use × hardness (gpg) = daily grains to remove.**
- Multiply by days between regenerations (commonly ~7) to find the capacity you need.
- Undersizing causes frequent regeneration and early wear; oversizing wastes money.
- Your exact **hardness number** (from a test) is the key input.

## Why "Grain Capacity" Is the Right Way to Size

A water softener removes hardness, measured in **grains**, until its resin is "full," then it **regenerates** (cleans itself) and starts over. A softener's **grain capacity** tells you how much hardness it can remove between regenerations. Size it too small and it regenerates constantly — wearing out faster and using more salt and water. Size it well and it regenerates efficiently, roughly weekly, and lasts. So sizing is really about matching capacity to how much hardness your household throws at it each day.

## The 3 Numbers You Need

1. **People in your home** — more people, more water, more hardness to remove.
2. **Daily water use per person** — a common planning figure is about **75 gallons per person per day.**
3. **Your water hardness** in grains per gallon (gpg) — get this from a water test. (If it's reported in ppm/mg/L, divide by 17.1 to get gpg.)

## The Formula

**Daily grains to remove = People × 75 gallons × Hardness (gpg)**

Then, to find the grain capacity you want (so it regenerates about weekly):

**Recommended capacity ≈ Daily grains × ~7 days**

Softeners come in standard sizes (commonly 24,000 / 32,000 / 40,000 / 48,000 / 64,000 grains), so you round up to the nearest practical size with a little headroom.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Family of 4, hardness 10 gpg:**
- Daily grains = 4 × 75 × 10 = **3,000 grains/day**
- Weekly = 3,000 × 7 = **21,000 grains**
- → A **32,000-grain** softener fits comfortably with headroom.

**Example 2 — Family of 4, very hard water 20 gpg (think Phoenix):**
- Daily grains = 4 × 75 × 20 = **6,000 grains/day**
- Weekly = 6,000 × 7 = **42,000 grains**
- → A **48,000-grain** softener is the right call.

**Example 3 — Couple, hardness 15 gpg:**
- Daily grains = 2 × 75 × 15 = **2,250 grains/day**
- Weekly = 2,250 × 7 = **15,750 grains**
- → A **24,000–32,000-grain** unit works well.

Notice how much **hardness** drives the result — the same family needs a bigger softener in very-hard-water Phoenix than in a moderately hard area. That's why your real hardness number matters so much.

## Don't Forget Iron and Special Cases

If your water also contains **iron** (common on wells), you add to the load — a common rule is to add several "grains" of demand per mg/L of iron, which can push you to a larger or specialized unit. High iron, very large households, or unusual usage patterns are exactly when a professional sizing (based on a real test) pays off.

**How do I calculate water softener grain capacity?**
Multiply people × 75 gallons per day × your hardness in gpg to get daily grains, then multiply by about 7 days for the capacity that regenerates roughly weekly.

It regenerates too often, uses more salt and water, wears out faster, and may not keep up with demand — letting hard water through.

## They Soften Water the Same Way

**Cons:**
- **Costs significantly more** per bag than salt.

## What's Actually Causing That Earthy Taste

The culprits have names: **geosmin** and **2-MIB (2-methylisoborneol)**. These are natural organic compounds produced by **algae and bacteria** in lakes, rivers, and reservoirs — the same source waters many cities draw from. Geosmin is literally the compound responsible for the smell of fresh soil and rain (it's why beets taste "earthy" too). Humans are astonishingly sensitive to it — we can detect geosmin and MIB at concentrations of just a few **parts per trillion**, which is why even a tiny amount makes your water taste noticeably musty.

So that "dirt" taste isn't dirt, and it isn't contamination in the harmful sense — it's a trace natural compound your palate is hardwired to notice.

## Why It Happens in Summer

There's a clear seasonal pattern, and it comes down to temperature. In **warm weather**, source-water reservoirs and lakes heat up, which fuels **algae blooms** — and more algae means more geosmin and MIB released into the water. Lake "turnover" (seasonal mixing of water layers) can also stir these compounds up. That's why complaints about musty, earthy tap water spike in **summer and early fall** and often fade as the water cools. If your water suddenly tastes earthy in July, this is almost certainly why.

## Is It Safe to Drink?

Yes. This is the most important reassurance: **geosmin and MIB are not considered health hazards** at the levels found in drinking water. They're a taste-and-odor nuisance, not a toxin. Your water utility is still treating and disinfecting your water to safety standards — the musty taste is an aesthetic problem riding along with otherwise-safe water. That said, "safe but unpleasant" is still unpleasant, and you don't have to put up with it.

## Why Your City Can't Always Fix It

You might wonder why the utility doesn't just remove it. The frustrating reality for water treatment plants is that **geosmin and MIB are notoriously difficult to remove with conventional treatment** — standard filtration and disinfection don't reliably eliminate them. Utilities often have to add **activated carbon** at the treatment plant during bloom season to knock the taste down, and even then, trace amounts can slip through to your tap. So a temporary musty taste isn't necessarily a sign your utility is failing — it's a sign these compounds are genuinely stubborn.
